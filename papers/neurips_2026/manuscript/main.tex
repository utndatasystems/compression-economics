\section{Who Is Actually Doing the Heavy Lifting in LLM-Based Compression?}

Strings are a core data type in production databases: in an Amazon Redshift fleet study, variable-length strings account for 52.1\% of stored columns and 53.8\% of predicate columns~\citep{vanrenen2024tpc}. Production text data, however, is heterogeneous and may not match an LLM’s training distribution, which is something general-purpose systems must therefore be able to cope with. As data accumulates, compression becomes especially important for cold storage, where space savings can be weighed favorably against access latency and encoding cost ~\citep{kipf2026waiting}. Yet favorable storage economics are not enough: a practical LLM codec must also meet broader systems requirement. Such a  Such a codec must preserve queryability, for example, supporting equality checks or joins without full decoding, while remaining robust to arbitrary inputs and degrading gracefully when model predictions are poor.

To this end, we study a layered architecture designed around the separation from tokenization and predictive compression. The tokenizer first maps strings to stable token IDs that act as a global dictionary and support operations over encoded data without model inference~\citep{schmidt2026accelerating}; these IDs form the lossless, queryable representation.  For cold storage or transport, an LLM predicts the token IDs and an arithmetic coder compresses them. Because tokenization already constitutes a lossless pre-compression, this predictive layer is optional. When it is used tough, arithmetic coding assigns shorter descriptions to more probable tokens ~\citep{witten1987arithmetic,steinruecken2015lossless}.

Prior LLM codecs do already combine next-token probabilities with arithmetic coding or other lossless coding schemes~\citep{valmeekam2023llmzip}. Prediction, however, is only once source of compression. Tokenization itself can also act as lossless pre-compression~\citep{deletang2024language}. The popular tokenization strategy BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding) even originated as a compression algorithm~\citep{gage1994new} in the first place, and its effectiveness has been linked to shorter token sequences~\citep{galle2019investigating}. From a systems perspective however, these gains ultimately only matter insofar as the complete codec improves on conventional alternatives such as Brotli~\citep{alakuijala2016brotli} and FSST~\citep{boncz2020fsst}.

What remains unclear is how much of an LLM codec's behavior comes from tokenization versus prediction. \citet{deletang2024language} compare ASCII and BPE~\citep{sennrich2016bpe} on natural text, but do not isolate these effects under inputs targeting realized end-to-end size. This distinction matters because prediction cost is measured in bits/token, whereas compression depends on bits/source byte: a poor probability model can still compress when tokens represent many bytes. We therefore isolate tokenization and prediction under adversarial inputs to determine when prediction failure becomes compression failure and when tokenization absorbs the cost.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We make two contributions: (1) we factor end-to-end compression into tokenizer fertility and predictive coding under natural, random, and adversarial inputs. This decomposition reveals that prediction failure need not imply compression failure: on text8, Qwen2.5-0.5B with arithmetic coding produces a complete stream at $17.6\%$ of raw size, and even when the MinProb adversary raises prediction cost by $5.68\times$, the stream remains compressive at $36.2\%$. (2) we formalize token- and byte-level adversarial objectives with realized-code accounting, showing when tokenization absorbs predictive surprise and when that protection against size expansion breaks down. Directly maximizing decoded-byte-normalized surprisal removes this buffer and expands the complete stream to $144.3\%$ of raw size.


\section{Compression Objectives and How to Construct Adversarial Inputs}
\label{sec:attacks}

A token can be extremely unlikely under the language model while decoding to few source bytes. We formalize the central distinction between prediction difficulty and end-to-end compression failure. We use one notation throughout: let $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$ be the generated token sequence, let $h_t=x_{<t}$ be its token history, let $\Dec = \operatorname{decode}$ denote the decoder, and let $p_\theta(v\mid h)$ be the language model probability of token $v$ after history $h$. Let $\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}$ denote the number of source bytes obtained after decoding the token sequence $x$. For example, if $\operatorname{decode}(x)=\texttt{hello}$, then $\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}=5$ bytes.
Let $B(x)$ be shorthand for the decoded source size in bytes: $B(x) = \bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}.$

The token-level objective, MinProb, selects the least-probable admissible continuation $\vtok \in G(h)$:
\begin{equation}
  \vtok
  = \arg\min_{v\in G(h)} p_\theta(v\mid h)
  = \arg\max_{v\in G(h)}
    \{-\log_2 p_\theta(v\mid h)\}.
  \label{eq:minprob}
\end{equation}

This is the LLM-centric token-level adversarial objective, which maximizes model surprisal and ideal coded bits/token equivalently, but not necessarily bits/source byte. To account for variable-length tokenization, define the incremental decoded byte length contributed by the token $v$,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta b(v;h)=
  \bytes{\operatorname{decode}(h\mathbin\Vert v)}-
  \bytes{\operatorname{decode}(h)}.
\end{equation}
The byte-aware MaxSurprisal/Byte objective instead selects $\vbyte$:
\begin{equation}
  \vbyte = {\arg \max}_{v\in G}
  \frac{-\log_2 p_\theta(v\mid h)}{\Delta b(v;h)}  \qquad [\mathrm{bits/byte}].
  \label{eq:spb}
\end{equation}
Unlike Eq.~\ref{eq:minprob}, it directly targets the ideal entropy-coding cost in bits/newly decoded source byte.
For a complete sequence $x$, the cumulative ideal model cost is the sequence NLL $L_\theta(x)$
\begin{equation}
  L_\theta(x)
  =
  \sum_{t=1}^{N}
  \ell_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
  =
  -\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log_2 p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
  \qquad [\mathrm{bits}],
  \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}
where any fixed initial context is implicit in $x_{<1}$. For serialized output
size $S_{\mathrm{codec}}(x)=\left|\Ser(x)\right|$ in bytes, the ideal and
realized source-normalized size ratios are
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{ideal}}(x)=100\,\frac{L_\theta(x)}{8B(x)}\ [\%]
  \qquad
  R_{\mathrm{real}}(x)=100\,\frac{S_{\mathrm{codec}}(x)}{B(x)}\ [\%].
  \label{eq:size-ratios}
\end{equation}
Values below $100\%$ denote compression; $R_{\mathrm{real}}$ additionally
captures arithmetic-coder, metadata, and framing effects.

\subsection{Prediction Loss Is Not Compression Loss}
\label{sec:objective-mismatch}

We define tokenizer fertility as the average decoded source-byte count:
%\todo{compare fertility accross different tokenizers }
\begin{equation} f(x)=\frac{\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}{N} \qquad [\mathrm{bytes/token}].,
\end{equation}
Using the sequence NLL $L_\theta(x)$, the ideal predictive rate is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\rm ideal}(x)=
  100\,\frac{L_\theta(x)}{8\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}
  =
  \underbrace{\frac{L_\theta(x)}{N}}_{\text{bits/token}}
  \underbrace{\frac{N}{\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}}_{\text{tokens/byte}}
  \frac{100}{8}.
  \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
Thus high bits/token can be offset by high bytes/token. Tokenization can buffer severe prediction failure when adversarial tokens represent many source bytes. Looking at \ref{eq:factorization}, hence, there is a compression boundary for $R_{ideal}$ that occurs at $\frac{L_\theta(x)}{N} < 8\,\frac{\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}{N} = 8 f(x)$. A token representing $f$ source bytes can tolerate up to $8f$ bits of surprisal before the predictive layer expands the source.

\subsection{Token- and Byte-Level Attacks}

\paragraph{Threat Model.}
We assume white-box access to the compression pipeline: the adversary can inspect the tokenizer and model logits and constructs length-$N$ adversarial inputs by autoregressive search over a candidate alphabet $G$. To ensure that generated token sequences survive decoding and re-tokenization, every prefix must satisfy $T(\operatorname{decode}(x_{1:t}))=x_{1:t}$; see Appendix~\ref{app:canonicality}.

\paragraph{Adversarial Input Generation.}
We benchmark natural Wikipedia text and random strings against two adversarial generation policies: \emph{Minimum probability} ($\vtok$) greedily maximizes Eq.~\ref{eq:minprob}, while \emph{maximum surprisal/byte} ($\vbyte$) greedily maximizes Eq.~\ref{eq:spb}. In addition, we provide a \emph{one-byte UTF-8 ablation} restricting the candidate alphabet $G$ to canonical tokens representing exactly one decoded byte, thereby removing variation in token fertility.
Under this constraint, the multi-byte buffering is removed and the $\vtok$ and $\vbyte$ objectives induce the same greedy ranking.  These inputs model adverse blocks arising from either benign data-model misalignment or malicious users. Among predictive objectives, MaxSurprisal/Byte ($\vbyte$) captures the relevant failure mode for end-to-end size because token-level loss alone does not determine serialized size.

%The \emph{one-byte UTF-8 ablation} is not a separate attack objective. It is an ablation of the candidate alphabet in which $G$ contains only canonical tokens contributing one decoded byte. This fixes fertility near one byte/token and removes the multi-byte-token buffering mechanism. Under an exact one-byte constraint, MinProb and SurprisalPerByte induce the same greedy ranking.

\paragraph{Natural-to-Random Shift Removes the Predictive Advantage.}
As was to be expected, Qwen+AC is the best performing compressor on natural text ($17.6\%$), all algorithms have acceptable performance. Interestingly, tokenization only is already a strong baseline, better than FSST even - this is explained by the fast that the Qwen tokenizer is trained on text (learned).
On random text, tokenization ... whereas prediction still repairs much of the Qwen token IDs baseline's inefficiency, but under this distribution shift it no longer beats a conventional codec. It is worth noting that random input alone is still not able to break the $100\%$ compression barrier for Qwen+AC.

%Thus prediction can substantially repair an inefficient token representation without necessarily yielding a competitive compressor.

\paragraph{Prediction Failure Does Not Imply Compression Failure.}
The MinProb ($\vtok$) attack increases prediction difficulty for the LLM $5.68\times$, yet remains compressive because tokenizer fertility is high at $7.09$ source bytes/token. This is because MinProb simultaneously affects tokenization: by selecting unlikely continuations directly in token space, it selects vocabulary items that frequently decode to long, multi-byte inputs, as reflected by the increase in fertility in Table~\ref{tab:decisive} (from 5.59 to 7.09). The Qwen token IDs baseline is slightly smaller than Qwen+AC on the same bytes, confirming that robustness comes from the multi-byte token representation rather than an additional predictive-coding gain. For Qwen token IDs, random input is in fact more difficult than MinProb input, underscoring that tokenization alone provides substantial pre-compression.

\paragraph{Attacking Fertility-Adjusted Cost Causes Expansion.}
Restricting MinProb to a one-byte token vocabulary is one way of removing the fertility buffer and makes the complete Qwen+AC stream expand to $122.8\%$. MaxSurprisal/Byte ($\vbyte$)
attacks the same buffer directly over the full vocabulary and likewise causes expansion and is the most effective joint attack on prediction + tokenization. MaxSurprisal/Byte ($\vbyte$) is the most effective attack on the complete Qwen + AC codec.



\section{Implications and Conclusion}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{LLM-Based Compression Survives Model Errors.} LLM compression is an end-to-end systems property, not merely a property of the language model: tokenization, prediction,
coding, and serialization jointly determine final size. On natural text (text8), the complete Qwen+AC reaches $17.6\%$ of raw size. MinProb ($\vtok$) raises
prediction cost to $5.68\times$ its text8 level, yet its payload remains at
$31.7\%$ because multi-byte tokens absorb the surprise; the Qwen token IDs baseline
is slightly smaller than Qwen+AC on the same MinProb input. Conversely, restricting
candidates to one-byte tokens removes the tokenization-buffer and makes the complete stream
expand to $122.8\%$. Compression therefore survives poor prediction because of
tokenizer fertility, not necessarily because the model predicts well.

\paragraph{Queryable Token Representation as the Foundation.} Token IDs provide a stable, queryable
representation for hot data, while predictive coding can be reserved for sufficiently cold blocks.
This separation matters because LLM compression trades storage savings for inference cost and
access latency, making access frequency and retention time central to whether it is worthwhile ~\citep{kipf2026waiting}. A conventional codec such as Brotli can serve as a fallback, ensuring that predictive
compression remains an optional optimization rather than a required representation.
