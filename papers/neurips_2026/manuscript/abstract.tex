Much of the data stored in real-world business systems is text. It has been shown that Large language models (LLMs) can act as powerful lossless compressors for text data especially: their next-token probabilities coupled with entropy coding can reduce data significantly below its original size and LLM-based prediction has been shown to outperform traditional compression schemes such as FSST and Brotli by up to $2\times$ on real-world data. Making this advantage practical though requires addressing inference cost, throughput, decompression, robustness, and economics; among these concerns, robustness remains understudied. We evaluate an LLM-based lossless compressor as a two-layer system of (1) tokenization and (2) prediction. We challenge/stress-test this system on adversarial inputs that approximately maximize prediction or compression cost mimicking  benign data--model misalignment and malicious inputs specifically designed to expand the encoded stream. Crucially, our analysis separates tokenization from LLM-prediction and shows that even severe prediction failure on the part of the LLM need not cause compression failure: unlikely tokens can decode to many source bytes and thereby buffer high prediction cost. Our byte-normalized adversary removes this protection: although MinProb raises prediction cost by $5.68\times$ while the complete stream remains at $36.2\%$ of raw size, MaxSurprisal/Byte expands it to $144.3\%$.
