\section{Who Is Actually Doing the Heavy Lifting in LLM-Based Compression?}

Strings are a core data type in production databases:~in an Amazon Redshift fleet study, variable-length strings account for 52.1\% of stored columns and 53.8\% of predicate columns~\citep{vanrenen2024tpc}.
Strings are typically user-supplied data that a system has no control over. A malicious actor could construct strings that expand on-disk storage significantly, leading to higher storage costs and unpredictable system performance.
Even data that is not intentionally malicious can be a problem: production data is heterogeneous and may not match an LLM’s training distribution, leading again to poor next-token predictions and thus poor compression ratios. As data accumulates, compression becomes especially important for cold storage, where space savings can be weighed favorably against access latency and encoding cost ~\citep{kipf2026waiting}. Yet favorable storage economics are not enough: a practical LLM codec must also meet broader systems requirements. Such a codec must preserve queryability, for example, supporting equality checks or joins without full decoding, while remaining robust to \emph{arbitrary} inputs and degrading gracefully when model predictions are poor.

To this end, we study a layered architecture designed around the separation between tokenization and predictive compression. The tokenizer first maps strings to stable token IDs that act as a global dictionary and support operations over encoded data without model inference~\citep{schmidt2026accelerating}; these IDs form the lossless, queryable representation.  For cold storage or transport, an LLM predicts the token IDs and an arithmetic coder~\cite{witten1987arithmetic} compresses them. Because tokenization already constitutes a lossless pre-compression, this predictive layer is optional. When used, arithmetic coding assigns shorter descriptions to more probable tokens ~\citep{witten1987arithmetic,steinruecken2015lossless}.

Prior LLM codecs do already combine next-token probabilities with arithmetic coding or other lossless coding schemes~\citep{valmeekam2023llmzip}. Prediction, however, is only one source of compression. Tokenization itself can also act as lossless pre-compression~\citep{deletang2024language}. Byte-Pair Encoding~\citep{sennrich2016bpe}, a popular tokenization strategy, originated as a compression algorithm ~\citep{gage1994new}, and its effectiveness has been linked to shorter token sequences~\citep{galle2019investigating}. From a systems perspective, however, these gains ultimately only matter insofar as the complete codec improves on conventional alternatives such as Brotli~\citep{alakuijala2016brotli} and FSST~\citep{boncz2020fsst}.

What remains unclear is how much of an LLM codec's behavior comes from tokenization versus prediction. \citet{deletang2024language} compare ASCII and BPE on natural text, but do not isolate these effects under inputs targeting realized end-to-end size. This distinction matters because prediction cost is measured in bits/token, whereas compression depends on bits/source byte: a poor probability model can still compress when tokens represent many bytes.
This fact is neglected in the widely known \MinProb{} attack that constructs adversarial inputs based on the sequence of lowest-probability tokens.
We therefore isolate tokenization and prediction under adversarial inputs to determine when prediction failure becomes compression failure and when tokenization absorbs the cost.

\sparagraph{Contributions.} We make two contributions: (1) We factor end-to-end compression into tokenizer fertility and predictive coding under natural, random, and adversarial inputs. This decomposition reveals that prediction failure need not imply compression failure: on the \texttt{text8} dataset, Qwen2.5-0.5B with arithmetic coding produces a complete stream at $17.6\%$ of raw size, and even when the \MinProb{} adversary raises prediction cost by $5.68\times$, the stream remains compressive at $36.2\%$. (2) We formalize token- and byte-level adversarial objectives with realized-code accounting, showing when tokenization absorbs predictive surprise and when that protection against size expansion breaks down. Directly maximizing decoded-byte-normalized surprisal with our \MaxSurprisal{} attack removes this buffer and expands the complete stream to $144.3\%$ of raw size.


\section{Compression Objectives and How to Construct Adversarial Inputs.}
\label{sec:attacks}

A token can be extremely unlikely under the language model while decoding to few source bytes. We formalize the central distinction between prediction difficulty and end-to-end compression failure. We use one notation throughout: let $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$ be the generated token sequence, $G$ the candidate token alphabet, $h_t=x_{<t}$ the sequence's token history, $\Dec = \operatorname{decode}$ denote the decoder, and $p_\theta(v\mid h)$ be the language model probability of token $v$ after history $h$. Let $\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}$ denote the number of source bytes obtained after decoding the token sequence $x$. For example, if $\operatorname{decode}(x)=\texttt{hello}$, then $\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}=5$ bytes.
Let $B(x)$ be shorthand for the decoded source size in bytes: $B(x) = \bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}.$

The token-level objective, \MinProb{}, selects the least-probable admissible continuation $\vtok \in G(h)$:
\begin{equation}
  \vtok \coloneq
     \arg\min_{v\in G(h)} p_\theta(v\mid h)
  = \arg\max_{v\in G(h)}
    \{-\log_2 p_\theta(v\mid h)\}.
  \label{eq:minprob}
\end{equation}

This is the LLM-centric token-level adversarial objective, which maximizes model surprisal and ideal coded bits/token equivalently, but not necessarily bits/source byte. To account for variable-length tokenization, define the incremental decoded byte length contributed by the token $v$,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta b(v;h) \coloneq
  \bytes{\operatorname{decode}(h\mathbin\Vert v)}-
  \bytes{\operatorname{decode}(h)}.
\end{equation}
The byte-aware \MaxSurprisal{} objective instead selects $\vbyte$:
\begin{equation}
  \vbyte \coloneq {\arg \max}_{v\in G}
  \frac{-\log_2 p_\theta(v\mid h)}{\Delta b(v;h)}  \qquad [\mathrm{bits/byte}].
  \label{eq:spb}
\end{equation}
Unlike Eq.~\eqref{eq:minprob}, it directly targets the ideal entropy-coding cost in bits/newly decoded source byte.
For a complete sequence $x$, the cumulative ideal model cost is the sequence Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) $L_\theta(x)$,
\begin{equation}
  L_\theta(x)
  =
  \sum_{t=1}^{N}
  \ell_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
  =
  -\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log_2 p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
  \qquad [\mathrm{bits}],
  \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}
where any fixed initial context is implicit in $x_{<1}$. For serialized output
size $S_{\mathrm{codec}}(x)=\left|\Ser(x)\right|$ in bytes, the ideal and
realized source-normalized size ratios are
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{ideal}}(x)=100\,\frac{L_\theta(x)}{8B(x)}\ [\%],
  \qquad
  R_{\mathrm{real}}(x)=100\,\frac{S_{\mathrm{codec}}(x)}{B(x)}\ [\%].
  \label{eq:size-ratios}
\end{equation}
Values below $100\%$ denote compression; $R_{\mathrm{real}}$ additionally
captures arithmetic-coder, metadata, and framing effects.

\subsection{Prediction Loss Is Not Compression Loss}
\label{sec:objective-mismatch}

We define tokenizer fertility as the average decoded source-byte count:

\begin{equation} f(x)=\frac{\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}{N} \qquad [\mathrm{bytes/token}].
\end{equation}
Using the sequence NLL $L_\theta(x)$, the ideal predictive rate is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\rm ideal}(x)=
  100\,\frac{L_\theta(x)}{8\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}
  =  \frac{100}{8}
  \underbrace{\frac{L_\theta(x)}{N}}_{\text{bits/token}}
  \underbrace{\frac{N}{\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}}_{\text{tokens/byte}}.
  \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
Thus high bits/token can be offset by high bytes/token. Tokenization can buffer severe prediction failure when adversarial tokens represent many source bytes. Looking at Eq.~\eqref{eq:factorization}, hence, there is a compression boundary for $R_{\rm ideal}$ that occurs at $\frac{L_\theta(x)}{N} = 8\,\frac{\bytes{\operatorname{decode}(x)}}{N} = 8 f(x)$. A token representing $f$ source bytes can tolerate up to $8f$ bits of surprisal before the predictive layer expands the source.

\subsection{Token- and Byte-Level Attacks}

\sparagraph{Threat Model.}
We assume white-box access to the compression pipeline: the adversary can inspect the tokenizer and model logits and construct length-$N$ adversarial inputs by autoregressive search over a candidate alphabet $G$. To ensure that generated token sequences survive decoding and re-tokenization, every prefix must satisfy $T(\operatorname{decode}(x_{1:t}))=x_{1:t}$; see Appendix~\ref{app:canonicality}.

\sparagraph{Adversarial Input Generation.}
We benchmark natural Wikipedia text and random strings against two adversarial generation policies: \MinProb ($ \vtok$) greedily maximizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:minprob}, while \MaxSurprisal  ($ \vbyte$) greedily maximizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:spb}. In addition, we provide a \emph{one-byte UTF-8 ablation} restricting the candidate alphabet $G$ to canonical tokens representing exactly one decoded byte, thereby removing variation in token fertility. Under this constraint, the multi-byte buffering is removed and the $\vtok$ and $\vbyte$ objectives induce the same greedy ranking.  These inputs model adverse blocks arising from either benign data-model misalignment or malicious users. Among the predictive objectives, \MaxSurprisal{} ($\vbyte$) directly targets end-to-end size expansion, whereas token-level loss alone does not determine serialized size.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate Qwen2.5-0.5B~\citep{qwen2024qwen25} on natural text, random text input, full-vocabulary adversarial
sequences, and a one-byte UTF-8 ablation. Each input in the attack evaluation contains 10,000 tokens, except for the full-vocabulary \MaxSurprisal{} ($\vbyte$) evaluation, which contains 1,024 tokens due to computational constraints.\footnote{This shorter evaluation does not change the qualitative conclusion; see Appendix~\ref{app:maxsurprisal-length}.}
All attacks use the same model, context, and arithmetic-coding configuration, and generated prefixes must round-trip through tokenizer decoding and re-encoding.

\sparagraph{Natural-to-Random Shift Removes the Predictive Advantage.} On natural text, Qwen+AC achieves the best compression ratio at $17.6\%$. Notably, tokenization alone is already a strong baseline, outperforming FSST, reflecting that Qwen's learned tokenizer is itself adapted to natural text. The switch to random input makes this advantage largely disappear: the Qwen token-ID representation expands to $172.1\%$ of the source size, and although prediction recovers much of this inefficiency, reducing Qwen+AC to $97.4\%$, it no longer outperforms Brotli. Nevertheless, random input alone is insufficient to make Qwen+AC expand beyond the original source size.

\sparagraph{Prediction Failure Does Not Imply Compression Failure.}
\MinProb{} ($\vtok$) raises LLM prediction cost by $5.68\times$, yet the stream remains compressive because tokenizer fertility rises to 7.09 source bytes/token. By selecting unlikely continuations in token space, \MinProb{} tends to choose vocabulary items that decode to long, multi-byte strings, increasing fertility from 5.59 to 7.09 (Table~\ref{tab:decisive}). On the same source bytes, Qwen token IDs are slightly smaller than Qwen+AC, indicating that this robustness comes from the multi-byte token representation rather than predictive coding. Indeed, random inputs are harder than \MinProb{} for the token-ID baseline, highlighting that tokenization provides substantial pre-compression.

\sparagraph{Attacking Fertility-Adjusted Cost Causes Expansion.}
Removing the fertility buffer translates weak LLM predictions into expansion. Restricting \MinProb{} to one-byte tokens only fixes fertility at 1 byte/token and expands Qwen+AC to $122.8\%$. \MaxSurprisal{} ($\vbyte$) attacks the same fertility buffer directly over the full vocabulary by maximizing surprisal per decoded byte; it likewise expands Qwen+AC (to $144.3\%$), making it the strongest end-to-end attack we evaluate.

\section{Implications and Conclusion} \label{sec:limitations}
\sparagraph{How LLM-Based Compression Survives Model Errors.} LLM compression is an end-to-end systems property:~tokenization, prediction, coding, and serialization jointly determine final size. Qwen+AC compresses natural text to $17.6\%$ of raw size and remains compressive under a $5.68\times$ increase in prediction cost, but expands once tokenizer fertility is removed or explicitly targeted. Thus, prediction loss alone does not determine compression failure; tokenizer fertility can absorb substantial model error. More broadly, our results suggest that benign text-model misalignment need not cause catastrophic compression failure as long as the input remains efficiently represented by the tokenizer: severe LLM misprediction can be tolerated when each predicted token still covers sufficiently many source bytes.
